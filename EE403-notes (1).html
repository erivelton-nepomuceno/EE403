1. Introduction to Optimisation

1.1 What is Optimisation?

  • Optimisation deals with the following problem – choose the value x such that some quantity F, which depends on x, is a minimum. Here, F is referred to as the objective function.

  • In mathematical terms, we express this problem as: \(\min_{x}F(x)\)

  • In seeking the minimum of F(x) we may often be restricted in the choice of x. Typically, there may be other quantities Ci(x) whose value must be maintained within certain limits. These are called constraints.

  • The variable x may be a scalar or an n-vector (i.e. x = [x1 x2 … xn]T).

  • Notice that vectors are represented with bold font, while scalars are not. Also, matrices are generally represented with bold capital font. Alternatively, vectors can be represented using an underline, i.e. x.

  • Hence, the full expression of the problem in standard form is as follows:

min F(x), x ∈ ℜn

Page 6

subject to Ci(x) = 0; i = 1, 2, … , k (equality constraints)

Cj(x) ≥ 0; j = k+1, k+2, … , m (inequality constraints)

where m represents the total number of constraints.

  • Note that if m = 0, then we are dealing with an unconstrained optimisation problem, otherwise, if m ≠ 0, then we have a constrained optimisation problem.

  • Furthermore, note that the maximum of a function F(x) is the same as the minimum of the negative of the function, i.e. – F(x). In other words:

max F(x) ≡ min( -F(x) )

Ci(x) ≤ 0 ≡ -Ci(x) ≥ 0

Engineering Examples of Optimisation

  • In its broadest sense, optimisation can be applied to solve almost any (if not all) engineering problems.

  • Some typical applications that use optimisation include:

  • Design of aircraft and aerospace structures for minimum weight.

  • Design of water resources systems for maximum benefit.

  • Design of pumps, turbines and heat transfer equipment for maximum efficiency.

  • Optimal design of electrical networks.

  • Selection of a site for an industry.

  • Analysis of statistical data and building empirical models from experimental results to obtain the most accurate representation of the physical phenomenon.

  • Allocation of resources or services among several activities to maximise benefit.

  • Controlling the waiting and idle times and queueing in production lines to reduce the costs.

  • Optimum design of control systems.

  • These example applications clearly illustrate the wide scope of optimisation!

  • Consider the following arbitrary graph, where all points shown are stationary points, i.e. the differential of the function at these points is zero.

  • The points A, C and E are all local minima. Each has a lower function value than any other point in its own neighbourhood. In mathematical terms, a local minimum x* exists for some small value δ if:

F(x*) ≤ F(x) for all x such that || x – x*|| < δ.

  • The point E is the global minimum in the range shown. In mathematical terms, a global minimum x* exists if:

F(x*) ≤ F(x) for all x.

  • Points B, D and F are all local maxima, with D being the global maximum in the range shown.

  • Finally, point G is referred to as a point of inflexion.

  • For this course, we make the following assumptions in relation to F(x):

(i) continuous

(ii) smooth – i.e. its first and second derivatives are continuous (i.e. point E can’t occur!)

(iii) single valued and dependent for every value of x, i.e. for each value of x there is one and only one corresponding value of F(x).

Basic optimality condition – 1 variable

  • Let x* denote the value of x at a local minimum.

  • Consider any value of x near x*, i.e. x = x* + h, where h is sufficiently small. Using Taylor’s Theorem we obtain the following expansion:

\(F(x^{*} + h) = F(x^{*}) + h\left. \ \frac{\text{dF}}{\text{dx}} \right|_{x^{*}} + \ \frac{1}{2}h^{2}\left. \ \frac{d^{2}F}{\text{d}\text{x}^{2}} \right|_{x^{*}} + \cdots\)

  • Therefore:

\(F\left( x^{*} + h \right) - F\left( x^{*} \right) = h\left. \ \frac{\text{dF}}{\text{dx}} \right|_{x^{*}} + \ \frac{1}{2}h^{2}\left. \ \frac{d^{2}F}{\text{d}\text{x}^{2}} \right|_{x^{*}} + \cdots\)

  • We know that F(x* + h) – F(x*) > 0 for a local minimum at x* given that |h|, i.e. |x – x*| , is sufficiently small.

  • Furthermore, we know that the first derivative at a minimum (or maximum) is zero.

  • Therefore, a sufficient condition for a local minimum is:

\(\left. \ \frac{\text{dF}}{\text{dx}} \right|_{x^{*}} = 0\) and \(\left. \ \frac{d^{2}F}{\text{d}\text{x}^{2}} \right|_{x^{*}} > 0\)

  • Page 13

  • Note that a global minimum is also a local minimum but there is no local criterion for verifying it. We would need to compare all local minimum points to determine which one is the global minimum in the range examined!

  • Example - determine the minimum point for the function f(x) = 3x3 + 9x2 – 4:

f(x) = 3x3 + 9x2 – 4

f ′(x) = 9x2 + 18x = 0 ⇒ x = 0 or -2

and f ′′(x) = 18x + 18

  • At x = 0, f ′′(x) = 18 > 0 which means that there’s a minimum point at x = 0.

  • At x = -2, f ′′(x) = -18 < 0 which means that the point x = -2 is not a minimum (it is in fact a maximum).

  • Determining f(x) at x = 0 gives f(0) = -4. Hence the function f(x) has a minimum point at (0, -4).

Basic optimality condition – n variables

  • Let x* be a local minimum. Then, at a point x* + h we have, by Taylor’s Theorem:

\(F(x^{*} + h) = F(x^{*}) + h^{T}\nabla F(x^{*}) + \frac{1}{2}h^{T}\nabla^{2}F(x^{*})h + \text{...}\)

  • Here, ∇F (Grad F) and ∇2F (Hessian Matrix of F) are given by:

\(\nabla F = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}} \\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{2}} \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{n}} \end{bmatrix}\) \(\nabla^{2}F = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{1}^{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{n}} \\ \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{2}^{2}} & & \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{n}\partial x_{1}} & & \cdots & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{n}^{2}} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Consider the case for a 3-dimensional function, i.e. n = 3. Hence x = [x1 x2 x3]T and:

\(F(x^{*} + h) = F(x^{*}) + \lbrack\begin{matrix} h_{1} & h_{2} & h_{3}\rbrack\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}} \\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{2}} \\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{3}} \end{bmatrix} \end{matrix}\)

\(+ \frac{1}{2}\lbrack\begin{matrix} h_{1} & h_{2} & h_{3} \end{matrix}\rbrack\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{1}^{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{3}} \\ \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{2}^{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{3}} \\ \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{3}\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{3}\partial x_{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{3}^{2}} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} h_{1} \\ h_{2} \\ h_{3} \end{bmatrix} + \text{...}\)

  • Page 15

  • Note that this can be expressed as follows:

\(F(x^{*} + h) = F(x^{*}) + \sum_{i = 1}^{3}{h_{i}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}}} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i = 1}^{3}{\sum_{j = 1}^{3}{h_{i}h_{j}\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}} + \text{...}}}\)

  • For ||h|| sufficiently small, F(x* + h) – F(x*) cannot be < 0, by definition of a local minimum.

  • Page 16

  • Hence, we can deduce that ∇F(x*) = 0, i.e. a vector of zeros. If this wasn’t the case, then we could choose a value of h that would make F(x* + h) – F(x*) negative!

  • Therefore the sign of F(x* + h) – F(x*) is determined by:

½ hT ∇2F(x*) h

  • Hence, to ensure a local minimum the term ½ hT ∇2F(x*) h must be ≥ 0 for all h for which ||h|| is sufficiently small.

  • Hence the Hessian matrix at x* must be positive definite.

  • Therefore, a sufficient condition for a local minimum for an n-variable continuous, differentiable function is:

∇F(x*) = 0 and ∇2F(x*) is positive definite.

  • Note that ∇2F is an n x n symmetric matrix because:

\(\nabla^{2}F_{ij} = \nabla^{2}F_{ji}\), i.e.: \(\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}} = \frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}\).

A note on positive definite matrices

  • A positive definite matrix P has the property:

zT P z > 0 if P is an n x n matrix and z is any non-null n-vector.

  • For example, consider the matrix A = \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), hence n = 2 and:

\(\lbrack\begin{matrix} z_{1} & z_{2} \end{matrix}\rbrack\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ z_{2} \end{bmatrix} = z_{1}^{2} + \text{2}\text{z}_{2}^{2} > \text{0 , }z \neq 0\)

  • Therefore A is positive definite.

  • Now consider B = \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\), hence n = 2 and:

\(\lbrack\begin{matrix} z_{1} & z_{2} \end{matrix}\rbrack\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ z_{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} z_{1} + \text{3}\text{z}_{2} & \text{2}\text{z}_{1} + \text{4}\text{z}_{2} \end{bmatrix}\ \begin{bmatrix} z_{1} \\ z_{2} \end{bmatrix}\)

\(= (z_{1}^{2} + \text{3}\text{z}_{1}z_{2}) + (\text{2}\text{z}_{1}z_{2} + \text{4}\text{z}_{2}^{2}) \)\[= z_{1}^{2} + \text{5}\text{z}_{1}z_{2} + \text{4}\text{z}_{2}^{2}\]

  • If we take z1 = -2 and z2 = 1, this gives a value of -2 which is < 0. Hence B is not a positive definite matrix.

  • Example - determine the minimum point(s) for the following function:

\(f(x) = x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2} + x_{3}^{2} - \text{4}\text{x}_{1} - \text{8}\text{x}_{2} - \text{12}\text{x}_{3} + \text{100}\)

\(\nabla f = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{1}} \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{2}} \\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{3}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \text{2}\text{x}_{1} - 4 \\ \text{2}\text{x}_{2} - 8 \\ \text{2}\text{x}_{3} - \text{12} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Solving ∇F = 0 gives:

x1 = 2, x2 = 4, x3 = 6

  • The Hessian matrix is given by:

\(\nabla^{2}f = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{1}^{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{1}\partial x_{3}} \\ \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{2}^{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{3}} \\ \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{3}\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{3}\partial x_{2}} & \frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{3}^{2}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\)

2. Line searches & functions of one variable

  • When it comes to minimising a function of several variables, we tend to use repeated minimisation of a function along search directions. In other words, minimising a multi-variable function involves repeated minimisation of the function for a single variable. We will look at this is more detail in due course.

  • Nevertheless, the fact remains that the procedure of finding the minimum of functions of one variable is an important element in any optimisation method.

  • We are now going to deal with such procedures in this section of the notes. In particular we are going to concentrate on two types of search methods – one based on fixed interval reductions (Grid, Fibonacci and Golden Section), the other based on curve fitting (Quadratic).

  • Note that these search methods are iterative approaches which continue until the minimum is found to be within the required degree of accuracy. As such, these methods are better suited for computer simulation!

  • This approach is useful in cases where:

(1) a mathematical expression for F(x) is unknown

Page 21

(2) calculating the solution of ∇F(x) = 0 proves exceedingly difficult!

  • In the case of (1) above, an example might be an industrial process that we do not have a mathematical expression for. However, we can still measure the output for given inputs by physically conducting the actual process itself.

  • This example highlights one of the key features of a good optimisation algorithm. Running the industrial process is timely and expensive and therefore each time we want to see the effect of an input on the industrial process means extra cost for the company involved.

  • Clearly, therefore, a good optimisation is one that can converge on the minimum in the least possible number of function evaluations.

  • Finally, note that all these search methods assume that we can locate a range [a b] in which a single local minimum exists. In practice, how do we find such an interval?

Finding interval containing minimum

  • The first step is to calculate F(x) at some point x = k.

  • Page 22

  • We then need to find a point b = k + h, where h is a positive step.

  • If F(k+h) > F(k), then we are moving away from the minimum and therefore, we now need to move in the opposite direction from k, i.e. we need to consider F(k-h).

  • If F(k-h) > F(k), then we have found a bracketing interval [k-h, k+h].

  • If not then we move further in the same direction, subtracting 2h each time we fail to establish an end for the bracketing interval, i.e. we consider F(k-3h), F(k-7h), etc.

  • If F(k+h) < F(k), however, then we continue moving in the same direction and consider F(k+3h), F(k+7h), etc… until we find a function value greater than F(k+h).

  • So, let us say that we find F(k+3h) > F(k+h), then the bracketing interval is [k k+3h].

2.1 The Grid method

  • This is the simplest method of determining the minimum of F(x) .

  • Page 23

  • We split the range [a b] into a number of subintervals of equal size. We then identify the subinterval which contains the minimum and repeat the process of subdividing.

  • This process continues until we obtain a subinterval of suitable accuracy.

2.2 The Fibonacci search

  • Page 27

  • Consider the case where we want to obtain the local minimum, i.e. an interval of suitable accuracy, and we have only n evaluations to do so.

  • Clearly it is unwise to use up all n evaluations on the first iteration. Instead, we should use the function evaluations we obtain on one iteration to determine the position of our next function evaluation. In order words, we learn information from one iteration and use this to improve our chances of locating the minimum on the next iteration.

  • Now consider the following diagram:

  • Page 28

  • Let us say that we know the minimum is located between the values x1 and x2 and that we know the value of F(x3) where x3 lies in the range [x1 x2].

  • If we were allowed to carry out one more function evaluation, where should we locate the point x4 in order to obtain the smallest possible interval in which the minimum is certainly located?

  • For convenience let x3 – x1 = L and x2 – x4 = R.

  • Now consider the following two cases:

(a) if F(x4) < F(x3) then the new interval for consideration will be [x1 x3] of length
x3 – x1 = L

(b) if F(x4) > F(x3) then the new interval for consideration will be [x4 x2] of length
x2 – x4 = R

  • Since we will not know in advance which of these two cases will occur, we choose the position of x4 so as to minimise the larger of L and R.

  • Page 29

  • We achieve this by simply making L = R, i.e.:

x3 – x1 = x2 – x4 or x4 = x1 + (x2 – x3).

  • Hence x4 is place symmetrically in the interval with respect to x3.

  • Any other position of x4 will result in either L < R or L > R and therefore can result in a new interval that is bigger than that obtained for L = R.

  • We can now repeat this process for each new interval until we obtain an interval that bounds the minimum within a certain acceptable limit.

  • So how do we determine the starting location for our first iteration? In other words how far from x1 do we place x3 (or, by symmetry, how far from x2 do we place x4)?

  • In order to determine this we need to consider the end point and work backwards.

  • Given that we want to obtain our local minimum with an error tolerance of less than ε, we need to obtain its estimate at the centre of the interval 2δ of size 2ε or less. Hence, our last useful interval can be represented as follows:

  • Page 30

  • Working backwards gives us the following pattern:

  • This gives us the sequence δ, δ, 2δ, 3δ, 5δ, 8δ, etc. Here the interval δ is obtained from half the last useful interval.

  • Page 31

  • The reason why 2δ is the last useful interval is because xn = xn-1 and therefore no new information regarding the minimum is obtained. In other words, which half of this interval is the minimum if F(xn) = F(xn-1)?

  • This sequence is the same as the Fibonacci sequence (hence the name of the search method) which is defined by:

Fi = Fi - 1 + Fi – 2, with F0 = F1 = 1

  • This gives the numbers: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, etc…

  • Therefore, given an interval [a b] and a final one of size δ, we can build the sequence:

δ, δ, 2δ, 3δ, 5δ, 8δ,…, Fiδ, …

  • Letting N be the number of iterations it takes to reduce the interval [a b] to the required interval δ, we can make:

FNδ = b – a

  • Hence: δ = \(\frac{b - a}{F_{N}}\)

  • Page 32

  • Thus, given a, b and ε, we finally need to determine the smallest Fibonacci number that satisfies:

δ ≤ ε

  • The internal points are then obtained using the following relationships:

xl = b - δFN-1 and xr = a + δFN-1

where a and b are the bounds of the current interval and xl and xr are the new internal
subdivision points.

2.3 The Golden Section search

  • Page 38

  • The Golden Section search is based on the same principles as the Fibonacci search. However it differs in the way in which the location of the intervals is chosen.

  • In this case, the intervals are reduced at each iteration by the same factor, τ. In order words:

\(\tau = \frac{F_{\text{i-1}}\delta}{F_{i}\delta}\)

where Fi δ is the length of one interval and Fi-1 δ is the length of the subsequent reduced
interval.

  • Since Fi = Fi - 1 + Fi – 2 (the Fibonacci sequence), we can state that:

FN = FN-1 + FN-2

  • Dividing across by FN-1 gives:

\(\frac{F_{N}}{F_{N - 1}} = 1 + \frac{F_{N - 2}}{F_{N - 1}}\) \(\Rightarrow \frac{1}{\tau} = 1 + \tau\)

  • Multiplying across by τ gives:

1 = τ + τ2 or τ2 + τ - 1 = 0

  • Page 39

  • Solving this equation (and considering only the positive root) we obtain:

τ = \(\frac{( - 1 + \sqrt{5})}{2} \approx 0.6180339887\)

  • This is known as the golden ratio, hence the name of the search method.

  • So how do we determine the new positions x1 and x2 in the first interval [a b]?

  • Refer to the following diagram. From symmetry we know that the reduced intervals equal each other, i.e.:

x2 – a = b – x1

  • We now know that each of these distances have to be τ (b – a), i.e. the golden ratio times the original interval length.

  • Page 40

  • Hence: x2 – a = τ (b – a) and b – x1 = τ (b – a)

  • This gives: x2 = a + τ (b – a) and x1 = b - τ (b – a)

  • These expressions are used for all subsequent intervals (with the appropriate substitutions – i.e. a and b are replaced with the new interval boundaries, etc…).

2.4 The Quadratic search (curve fitting approach) – 1 variable

  • This is usually done by evaluating F(x) for a number of values of x and fitting a quadratic function approximation, which can be minimised analytically.

  • The approximate minimum that results is used to improve the approximating function and a new minimum is found.

  • This process is repeated until a suitable minimum value is obtained.

  • Let us consider that a function F(x) is known at 3 points α, β and γ and has the values Fα, Fβ and Fγ at these points respectively.

  • Page 46

  • We approximate F(x) by the following quadratic polynomial:

Q(x) = Ax2 + Bx + C

  • Thus: Fα = Aα2 + Bα + C

Fβ = Aβ2 + Bβ + C

Fγ = Aγ2 + Bγ + C

  • Solving these equations for A, B and C gives:

\(A = \frac{(\text{γ}\text{-}\text{β})F_{\alpha} + (\alpha - \gamma)F_{\beta} + (\beta - \alpha)F_{\gamma}}{(\alpha - \beta)(\beta - \gamma)(\gamma - \alpha)}\)

\(B = \frac{(\beta^{2} - \gamma^{2})F_{\alpha} + (\gamma^{2} - \alpha^{2})F_{\beta} + (\alpha^{2} - \beta^{2})F_{\gamma}}{(\alpha - \beta)(\beta - \gamma)(\gamma - \alpha)}\)

\(C = \frac{\text{βγ}\text{ }(\text{γ}\text{-}\text{β})F_{\alpha} + \text{γα}\text{ }(\alpha - \gamma)F_{\beta} + \text{αβ}\text{ }(\beta - \alpha)F_{\gamma}}{(\alpha - \beta)(\beta - \gamma)(\gamma - \alpha)}\)

  • Since differentiating Q(x) gives 2Ax + B and differentiating again gives A, we can state that if A > 0, then Q(x) has a minimum at the point δ, where:

\(\delta = - \frac{B}{\text{2A}}\)

  • Substituting for A and B gives:

\(\delta = \frac{1}{2}\left\lbrack \frac{(\beta^{2}\text{-}\text{γ}^{2})F_{\alpha} + (\gamma^{2}\text{-}\text{α}^{2})F_{\beta} + (\alpha^{2}\text{-}\text{β}^{2})F_{\gamma}}{(\text{γ}\text{-}\text{β})F_{\alpha} + (\alpha - \gamma)F_{\beta} + (\beta - \alpha)F_{\gamma}} \right\rbrack\)

\(\delta = \frac{1}{2}(\alpha + \beta) + \frac{\frac{1}{2}(F_{\alpha} - F_{\beta})(\beta - \gamma)(\gamma - \alpha)}{(\beta - \gamma)F_{\alpha} + (\gamma - \alpha)F_{\beta} + (\alpha - \beta)F_{\gamma}}\)

3.3 The Simplex Method (Nelder and Mead)

  • The Simplex method is another direct search method but takes a very different approach to the Univariate and the DSC search methods when it comes to determining the sequence of one-dimensional search directions.

  • The original Simplex method was derived by Spendley, Hext and Himsworth but was later improved upon by Nelder and Mead.

  • First off, a simplex in n-dimensional space is a polygon with n+1 vertices.

  • In 2-D space, the simplex is a triangle. In other words, the three points must not lie on a straight line. Similarly, in 3-D space, the 4 points must not lie on the same plane, etc.

  • The Simplex method evaluates the function at each of the vertices in the simplex.

  • A new point is then generated, and the function is evaluated at this point. The point with the largest function value is rejected and the new point is subsequently introduced into the simplex.

  • Page 103

  • In this fashion, a sequence of new simplices is created, with the best point in the simplex converging to a local minimum.

  • The generation of the new point is obtained by the application of three basic operations – namely reflection, expansion and contraction.

  • The Simplex algorithm is now outlined using a 2-D example. In other words we will use a triangle (with n = 2) to illustrate the operation of the Simplex method.

  • Consider the 3 points, xlow, xm, xh of a simplex in 2-D space as shown below. The choice of this initial simple is entirely arbitrary.

  • Page 104

  • Step 1 - We calculate the function at each of these points and rank them from the lowest to the highest. Let’s say that:

F(xlow) ≤ F(xm) ≤ F(xh)

  • Find the centroid, say xg, of all the points, except xh ( i.e. the one giving the highest function value):

xg = \(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \neq h}^{}x_{i}\)

  • Step 2 - Reflection - A new point is generated by reflecting xh about the centroid, as illustrated below:

  • The reflected point xr satisfies the condition:

xh – xg = xg - xr

or xr = 2xg – xh

  • Page 105

  • We want to find a point that is better than xm.

  • If F(xr) ≥ F(xm) then it’s possible that we have moved too far out of the simplex and a better point may be found by making a contraction – go to step 3.

  • If, instead, F(xm) > F(xr) > F(xl) then we have found an improvement on xm. We therefore replace the worst point xh with xr and return to step 1.

  • If, finally, F(xr) < F(xlow) then we have found the best point to date. At this stage we make the reasonable assumption that a larger step beyond xr may lead to an even better point:

  • Page 106

  • This expanded point xe is obtained so that:

xe – xg = μ (xr – xg), with μ > 1

or xe = xg (1 - μ) + μ xr

  • Now, if F(xe) < F(xr) then replace the worst point xh with xe and return to step 1.

  • Otherwise xr is the better point and therefore replace xh with xr and return to step 1.

  • Page 107

  • Step 3 - Contraction – There are two possible scenarios:

  • Scenario 1: If it turns out that F(xr) ≥ F(xh) then we look for a point xc, located on the line joining xh and xg, that is better than the point xr:

  • We make a contraction of the original simplex with the condition:

xc – xg = η(xh – xg), with 0 < η < 1

or xc = xg (1 - η) + ηxh

  • Now, if F(xc) < F(xh) we replace xh with xc and go to step 1. Otherwise we go to step 4.

  • Page 108

  • Scenario 2: If F(xr) < F(xh), we look for a point xc, located on the line joining xg and xr:

  • The point xc now satisfies the condition:

xc – xg = η(xr – xg), with 0 < η < 1

or xc = xg (1 - η) + ηxr

  • Now, if F(xc) < F(xr) we replace xh with xc and go to step 1, otherwise go to step 4. (Note, finding F(xc) < F(xh) is pointless considering that we know that F(xr) < F(xh) at this stage!)

  • Page 109

  • Step 4 - Reduction of Simplex – To reach this step, reflection, expansion and contraction have all failed to return a point better than xh.

  • Therefore, it appears that the minimum is enclosed by the current simplex.

  • We now contract the simplex around the best point by reducing each side by half.

  • In order words, each point in the simplex, xi is replaced by: \(\frac{x_{i} + x_{\text{low}}}{2}\)

  • Graphically:

  • Page 110

  • We then proceed to step 1 and repeat the process.

  • Note the expansion factor is typically taken as μ = 2, while the contraction factor is typically η = ½ .

Convergence

  • For each new simplex we should test for convergence before continuing. One possible criterion is that the simplex satisfies:

\(\sum_{i = 1}^{n + 1}\left| F(x_{i}) - F(x_{\text{low}}) \right|\) < ε

where ε is a positive small value.

  • This criterion establishes a simplex having a deviation from the best obtained value less than ε.

  • If this criterion is met, then xlow is taken to be the estimate of the minimum and the simplex algorithm is terminated.

4. Gradient Methods

  • Gradient algorithms not only make use of values of the function F in determining search directions, but also make use of values of the gradient vector ∇F.

  • Of course, this is only useful in cases where the first derivatives are easily calculated.

  • In this section of the notes, we are going to examine a few different gradient methods.

4.1 Steepest Descent

  • The most obvious direction in which to search for a minimum is along the direction of steepest descent.

  • The gradient of the function defines the steepest ascent, hence the steepest descent is given by:

-∇F(x)

  • Page 121

  • This property can be proved as follows. We will consider the case of a 2-variable function, but the principle is easily extended to functions of n variables.

  • Consider a two-variable function F(x) which has the following first derivatives at the point x:

∇F(x) = \(\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}} \\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{2}} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Now consider a two-dimensional vector d = [d1 d2]T and examine the effect of moving a small distance α in the direction of d from the point x to the new point x + αd.

Steepest Descent Algorithm

  • The steepest descent (or gradient descent) algorithm works in the same manner as the direct search methods but in this case, we search along the line given by:

x(k+1) = x(k) - α∇F(x(k))

  • Convergence, in this case, is given by either ||x(k+1) – x(k)|| < ε1 or || g(k+1)|| < ε2. In the latter case, if the gradient is very small, than you are effectively no longer descending!

4.2 The Conjugate Gradient Algorithm

  • First, let us analyse the behaviour of the univariate search algorithm on the general spherical function in n-dimensions, i.e.:

F(x) = a + bTx + ½ xTx

where x is a n-dimensional vector.

  • Note, this is a specific case of the more general quadratic function, given by:

a + bTx + ½ xTG x

Page 137

with the Hessian matrix G = I

  • Expanding F(x) we get:

F(x) = a + b1x1 + b2x2 + … + bnxn + ½ ( x12 + x22 + … + xn2)

⇒ F(x) = a + (b1x1 + ½ x12) + (b2x2 + ½ x22) + … + (bnxn + ½ xn2)

  • This expression highlights the fact that we can minimise F(x) by choosing values of x1, x2, …, xn separately so as to minimise each bracketed term independently.

  • For example:

\(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\) (b1x1 + ½ x12) = b1 + x1 = 0 ⇒ x1 = -b1

  • Page 138

  • Similarly, x2 = -b2, etc…

  • In other words, we can search along each axis independently and in any order, finding the minimum in n steps.

  • This is because the Hessian matrix, the identity matrix in this case, has no off-diagonal terms, i.e. the off-diagonal terms are all zero.

  • Now consider the general quadratic function, with a positive definite G:

F(x) = a + bTx + ½ xTG x

  • Let the vectors t1, t2, … tn be a set of search directions and let us define the matrix T as:

T = [t1 t2 … tn]

  • We now define a new vector y = [y1 y2 … yn]T, so that: x = Ty

  • Page 139

  • As long as T is non-singular (i.e. ti is not linearly dependent), this results in a 1-to-1 correspondence between x and y.

  • Thus:

F(y) = a + bTTy + ½ yT TT G T y

where:

TT G T = \(\begin{bmatrix} {t_{1}}^{T} \\ {t_{2}}^{T} \\ \vdots \\ {t_{n}}^{T} \end{bmatrix}G\begin{bmatrix} t_{1} & t_{2} & \cdots & t_{n} \end{bmatrix}\) = \(\begin{bmatrix} {t_{1}}^{T}Gt_{1} & {t_{1}}^{T}Gt_{2} & \cdots & {t_{1}}^{T}Gt_{n} \\ {t_{2}}^{T}Gt_{1} & {t_{2}}^{T}Gt_{2} & & \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ {t_{n}}^{T}Gt_{1} & & \cdots & {t_{n}}^{T}Gt_{n} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • If we can obtain ti so that:

tiTG tj = 0, i ≠ j

then TT G T will have zero off-diagonal terms, i.e.:

TT G T = \(\begin{bmatrix} d_{\text{11}} & & & 0 \\ & d_{\text{22}} & & \\ & & \ddots & \\ 0 & & & d_{\text{nn}} \end{bmatrix}\) = D

  • Page 140

  • Vectors that have the property tiTG tj = 0, i ≠ j are said to be conjugate with respect to G.

  • Note that the vectors ti are not necessarily orthogonal unless G happens to be a unit matrix.

  • Letting bTT = c = [c1 c2 … cn]T, we can express F(y) as:

F(y) = a + cTy + ½ yT D y

⇒ F(y) = a + (c1y1 + ½ d11y12) + (c2y2 + ½ d22y22) + … + (cnyn + ½ dnnyn2)

  • The minimum of F(y) can now be found by minimising with respect to y1, y2, …, yn separately in any order in n steps. For eg.:

\(\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{1}}\) (c1y1 + ½ d11y12) = c1 + d11y1 = 0 ⇒ y1 = -\(\frac{c_{1}}{d_{\text{11}}}\)

  • Similarly, y2 = -\(\frac{c_{2}}{d_{\text{22}}}\), etc…

  • Page 141

  • An alternative way to view this is that we can search for a minimum along each direction t1, t2, … tn independently because:

x = Ty = y1 t1 + y2 t2 + … yn tn

  • Hence, changing yi is equivalent to moving along the line: x + yi ti

  • Therefore finding the minimum of F(y) with respect to yi is equivalent to finding the minimum along ti. The value yi determines how far along the direction ti we travel.

  • Thus, any optimisation algorithm which generates search directions which are conjugate with respect to the Hessian matrix of a n-dimensional quadratic function can find the minimum in n steps.

  • Page 142

  • Conjugate search directions are also useful on non-quadratic functions because, close to a local minimum, most smooth continuous functions can be closely approximated by a quadratic and hence fast final convergence can be achieved.

4.2.1 Conjugate Gradient or Fletcher-Reeves method

  • The Fletcher-Reeves method works as follows.

  • As before, we let x(0) be the starting point and p(k) the search direction from x(k).

  • We set the first search direction to be the steepest descent:

p(0) = -∇F(x(0)) = -g(0)

  • Page 148

  • For convenience, we denote g = ∇F(x). Hence p(0) = -g(0).

  • We then set:

x(1) = x(0) + α p(0)

  • We choose α to minimise the function F(x) along p(0) and thus obtain x(1).

  • Now we deviate from the steepest descent approach, by setting the next search direction p(1) as:

p(1) = - g (1) + β (1)p(0)

where:

β (1) = \(\frac{{g^{(1)}}^{T}g^{(1)}}{{g^{(0)}}^{T}g^{(0)}}\)

  • In general, for all search directions except for p(0), we have:

p(k+1) = - g (k+1) + β (k+1)p(k)

where:

β (k+1) = \(\frac{{g^{(k + 1)}}^{T}g^{(k + 1)}}{{g^{(k)}}^{T}g^{(k)}}\)

  • Page 149

  • We then repeat the line search using this new search direction. The process continues until we converge to a suitable value - either ||x(k+1) – x(k)|| < ε1 or || g(k+1)|| < ε2.

  • Conjugate gradients is therefore similar to steepest descent, but at each iteration the search direction has a multiple of the previous search direction added to the steepest descent direction.

  • As noted already, conjugate gradient has n-step quadratic convergence.

  • On non-quadratic functions, we reset the search direction to steepest descent after every n iterations.

  • Page 150

  • The key points of this particular method is that it is relatively simple and, moreover, the conjugate directions for a quadratic function are determined without actually calculating the Hessian matrix.

  • Hence it requires the storage of only one vector, p(k-1) as well as x(k) and p(k). As a result, this method is often used on very large problems (n > 100).

  • A drawback of this method is that the function must be accurately minimised along each search direction. This is expensive in terms of function evaluations.

Short Proof – Fletcher-Reeves generates conjugate directions

  • Before looking at an example, we will now prove that the Fletcher-Reeves algorithm generates conjugate directions on two-dimensional quadratic functions.

  • The 2-d quadratic function is given by: F(x) = a + bTx + ½ xT H x

  • The gradient of this function at point x is given by:

g = ∇F(x) = b + Hx

  • Hence:

Page 151

g(0) = b + Hx(0) and g(1) = b + Hx(1) (equations X1 for reference)

  • The search direction p(0) is given by:

p(0) = -g(0) = -(b + Hx(0))

  • The new point is at:

x(1) = x(0) + α p(0) (equation X2 for reference)

  • The next search direction is then :

p(1) = - g (1) + β (1)p(0) = - g (1) - β (1)g(0), with β (1) = \(\frac{{g^{(1)}}^{T}g^{(1)}}{{g^{(0)}}^{T}g^{(0)}}\)

  • Page 152

  • Thus, in order to prove that conjugate search directions are generated, we need to show that:

\({p^{(1)}}^{T}\)H p(0) = 0

  • We know that:

\({p^{(1)}}^{T}\)H p(0) = (- g (1) - β (1)g(0))T H p(0)

  • Rewriting equation X2 gives:

p(0) = \(\frac{1}{\alpha}\) (x(1) – x(0))

  • Therefore:

\({p^{(1)}}^{T}\)H p(0) = \(\frac{1}{\alpha}\) (- g (1) - β (1)g(0))T H (x(1) – x(0))

  • From equations X1, we can show that:

H (x(1) – x(0)) = g(1) – g(0)

  • Therefore:

\({p^{(1)}}^{T}\)H p(0) = \(\frac{1}{\alpha}\) (- g (1) - β (1)g(0))T (g(1) – g(0))

  • Page 153

  • Multiplying out the RHS gives:

\({p^{(1)}}^{T}\)H p(0) = \(\frac{1}{\alpha}\) (-\({g^{(1)}}^{T}\)g (1) + \({g^{(1)}}^{T}\)g (0) - β (1)\({g^{(0)}}^{T}\)g(1) + β (1)\({g^{(0)}}^{T}\)g(0))

  • Since x(1) is the position of the minimum of F(x) along p(0), and we know that the gradient at this point, i.e. g(1), is orthogonal to the gradient that resulted in x(1) in the first place, i.e. g(0), then:

\({g^{(1)}}^{T}\)g (0) = 0

  • Hence:

\({p^{(1)}}^{T}\)H p(0) = \(\frac{1}{\alpha}\) (-\({g^{(1)}}^{T}\)g (1) + β (1)\({g^{(0)}}^{T}\)g(0))

  • Page 154

  • Substituting for β (1) gives:

\({p^{(1)}}^{T}\)H p(0) = 0

  • Thus, the search directions p are mutually conjugate and so the Fletcher-Reeves algorithm generates conjugate search directions in two dimensions.

  • The prove this for n-dimensions is a little more complicated! – refer to a good textbook for detail!

5. Hessian-based Gradient Methods

  • We are now going to study some gradient methods that make use of both the first and second derivatives of F(x).

5.1 Newton’s Method

  • Let x(k) be the current estimate of a minimum of the function F(x).

  • Now, using Taylor’s Theorem, consider the quadratic approximation to F(x), i.e. Q(x), about this point:

Q(x) ≈ F(x(k)) + (x – x(k))T g(k) + ½ (x – x(k))T H(k)(x – x(k))

where:

g(k) = ∇F(x(k)) and H(k) = ∇2F(x(k))

  • An improved estimate of the actual minimum can be obtained by finding the minimum of this approximate quadratic approximation.

  • Page 160

  • Differentiating the quadratic approximation gives:

∇Q(x) ≈ g(k) + H(k)(x – x(k))

  • If we denote the minimum of the quadratic approximation as x*, then

g(k) + H(k)(x* – x(k)) = 0

⇒ H(k)(x* – x(k)) = - g(k)

  • Let δx(k) = x* – x(k), i.e. δx(k) is an estimate of the correction to be applied to x(k) in order to arrive at the minimum. Hence:

H(k) δx(k) = - g(k)

⇒ δx(k) = - \({\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{(k)}}}^{\mathbf{-}\mathbf{1}}\)g(k)

  • Thus an improved estimate of the actual minimum of F(x) is given by:

x(k+1) = x(k) + δx(k)

or x(k+1) = x(k) - \({\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{(k)}}}^{\mathbf{-}\mathbf{1}}\)g(k)

  • Page 161

  • Note that the hessian matrix H(k) is assumed positive definite. If it wasn’t, then the approximating function has no minimum!

  • This approach is called Newton’s Method.

  • On a positive definite quadratic function, Newton’s method has a 1-step quadratic convergence. In other words, it will always find the minimum in 1 step, regardless of the starting point.

  • On non-quadratic functions, it is typically used to generate search directions, with one-dimensional searches along these directions.

Newton’s Algorithm for Quadratic Functions

  • Page 162

  • Select a starting point x(0).

  • Solve the following set of equations to get p(0):

H(0)p(0) = - g(0)

  • The minimum is then:

xmin = x(0) + p(0)

Newton’s Algorithm for General Functions

  • Select a starting point x(0) and evaluate F(x(0)).

  • Starting with k = 0, solve the following set of equations to get p(k):

H(k)p(k) = - g(k)

  • Page 163

  • Using a line search procedure, solve:

\(\underset{\alpha}{\text{min}}\)F(x(k) + αp(k)) to give α(k)

  • Check for convergence. If ||x(k+1) – x(k)|| is sufficiently small or if F(x(k+1)) - F(x(k)) is sufficiently small, then stop. Otherwise, set k = k+1 and repeat the process.

Illustrative Example (Quadratic Function)

  • Consider the quadratic function given by:

f(x) = x1 + x2 + x22 + 0.5x1x2 + 0.5x12

  • Starting with the point x(0) = [2 2]T, use Newton’s method to obtain the minimum of the above function.

  • Page 164

  • The gradient of the function is given by:

g = ∇f(x) = \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 + \text{0.5}\text{x}_{2} + x_{1} \\ 1 + \text{2}\text{x}_{2} + \text{0.5}\text{x}_{1} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Calculate at x(0) we get:

g(0) = ∇f(x(0)) = \(\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\)

  • The hessian matrix is given by:

H = ∇2f(x) = \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Clearly the hessian matrix is independent of the starting point and therefore is the same for all arbitrary starting points! Hence:

H(0) = ∇2f(x(0)) = \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Page 165

  • Solving the set of equations H(0)p(0) = - g(0) gives:

p(0) = \(\begin{bmatrix} - \frac{\text{20}}{7} \\ - \frac{\text{16}}{7} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Hence, as in the previous examples, the minimum is:

xmin = x(0) + p(0) ⇒ \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}\) + \(\begin{bmatrix} - \frac{\text{20}}{7} \\ - \frac{\text{16}}{7} \end{bmatrix}\) = \(\begin{bmatrix} - \frac{6}{7} \\ - \frac{2}{7} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Page 166

  • As already stated, Newton’s method has a 1-step convergence on positive definite quadratic functions.

  • Furthermore, the search directions are downhill if H(k) is positive definite. This is because the slope of F along p(k) is:

\({p^{(k)}}^{T}g^{(k)} = - {g^{(k)}}^{T}{H^{(k)}}^{- 1}g^{(k)}\)

Note: (Hg)T = gTHT and if H is symmetric, then HT = H

  • Since H(k) is taken to be positive definite, so too is \({H^{(k)}}^{- 1}\). Note a positive definite matrix has a positive definite inverse.

  • Hence the slope of F is negative, i.e. downhill, unless g(k) = 0, in which case, the search direction p(k) = 0.

  • Unfortunately Newton’s method has several disadvantages also:

  • It requires the second derivatives to be computed. This is expensive (especially for large n, as the effort is proportional to n2) and inaccurate unless analytical formulae are available.

  • Page 167

  • A system of linear equations must be solved on each iteration. This can be costly for problems with large dimensions.

  • The Hessian matrix H(k) at x(k) may be singular (i.e. non-invertible!) even if F(x) has a local minimum. Hence the method fails to compute a search direction.

  • H(k) may not be positive definite (i.e. uphill search direction produced).

  • Therefore Newton’s method is not much use, except for small problems (n ≤ 5 say), preferably with analytic expressions for the second derivatives! Also, knowledge of a good starting point is useful.

  • However, due to its good ultimate convergence, Newton’s method does provide the incentive for more efficient algorithms for unconstrained optimisation.

5.2 Quasi-Newton or Variable Metric Methods

  • Suppose we know n+1 values of the gradient, say g(0), g(1), …, g(n), corresponding to the n+1 points x(0), x(1), …, x(n) on a positive definite quadratic function Q(x).

  • Page 168

  • Then we can compute the Hessian matrix as follows:

  • We know that: Q(x) = a + bTx + ½xTHx and g = b + Hx

  • Then: g(k) – g(k - 1) = H(x(k)- x(k - 1))

  • Denoting λ(k) = g(k) – g(k - 1) and δ(k) = x(k)- x(k - 1), we have: λ(k) = H δ(k)

  • Hence:

λ(1) = H δ(1)

λ(2) = H δ(2)

λ(n) = H δ(n)

  • We can express this set of equations as:

K = H ∆

Page 169

where K = {λ(1), λ(2), …, λ(n)} and ∆ = {δ(1), δ(2), …, δ(n)}

  • Hence, we can calculate H as:

H = K ∆-1 or H-1 = ∆ K -1

  • Thus, we can use Newton’s method to obtain the minimum of the quadratic function Q(x) in one step using:

x(n+1) = xmin = x(n) - ∆ K -1g(n)

  • Hence, we have computed a Newton step without having explicitly calculated the Hessian matrix.

  • However, this approach still involves solving a set of linear equations. Furthermore, n+1 calculations of the gradient are required before we obtain the second derivative.

  • Page 170

  • Clearly, this approach is not very practical but it is useful as a basic idea from which to derive better, more efficient methods.

  • Better methods have been devised which use the λ(k) and δ(k) vectors in a more efficient manner. These methods estimate the inverse of the Hessian, using formulae that improve this estimate with each iteration.

  • These methods are called the Quasi-Newton or Variable Metric methods.

Standard Approach:

  • Recall that the Newton’s Method produces the search direction:

p(k) = - \({\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{(k)}}}^{\mathbf{-}\mathbf{1}}\)g(k)

  • The steepest descent method, from earlier, produces the search direction:

p(k) = - g(k) = - I g(k)

  • Page 171

  • Both these approaches produce search directions by multiplying the gradient vector by a matrix (albeit an implicit identity matrix in the latter case!).

  • We can view these methods as examples of a general class of methods that generates search directions of the form:

p(k) = - M(k) g(k)

  • The Quasi-Newton methods produce a sequence of matrices M(0), M(1), …, M(k), which are generated by updating M(i) to give M(i+1). Each of these are used as a substitute for \({\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{(k)}}}^{\mathbf{-}\mathbf{1}}\).

  • The updating process at x(k) involves only the use of M(k-1), λ(k) and δ(k) and is designed to ensure that the following condition (a property of \({\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{(k)}}}^{\mathbf{-}\mathbf{1}}\)) is satisfied:

M(k) λ(k) = δ(k)

  • This can be achieved in a number of ways and as such there are various Quasi-Newton methods. The original one is associated with the names Davidon, Fletcher and Powell, and we refer to their method as the DFP algorithm.

5.2.1 The DFP Algorithm

  • Step 1: Choose an arbitrary starting point x(0).

  • Choose a positive definite n x n matrix M(0). In practice, M(0) is typically chosen as the identity matrix, (hence the first search direction is the steepest descent), i.e.:

M(0) = I

  • Compute the gradient vector g(0).

  • Stop if || g(0)|| < ε1. Otherwise, set k = 0 and continue. Here ε1 represents an acceptable tolerance value, specified at the outset of the algorithm.

  • Step 2: Calculate the search direction:

p(k) = - M(k) g(k)

  • Page 173

  • Step 3: Compute the new point x(k+1) by searching for the minimum along the direction p(k). In other words, determine αmin(k) to minimise F(x(k) + α(k) p(k)) and set:

x(k+1) = x(k) + αmin(k) p(k)

  • Step 4: Determine the vector:

δ(k+1) = x(k+1)- x(k )

  • Stop if || δ(k+1) || < ε2. Otherwise, continue! Here ε2 represents an acceptable tolerance value, specified at the outset of the algorithm.

  • Calculate the new gradient vector, g(k+1). Stop if || g(k+1)|| < ε1. Otherwise determine the vector:

λ(k+1) = g(k+1) – g(k)

  • Step 5: Compute M(k+1) as follows:

A(k+1) = \(\frac{\delta^{(k + 1)}{\delta^{(k + 1)}}^{T}}{{\delta^{(k + 1)}}^{T}\lambda^{(k + 1)}}\)

B(k+1) = \(- \left( \frac{M^{(k)}\lambda^{(k + 1)}{\lambda^{(k + 1)}}^{T}M^{(k)}}{{\lambda^{(k + 1)}}^{T}M^{(k)}\lambda^{(k + 1)}} \right)\)

M(k+1) = M(k) + A(k+1) + B(k+1)

  • Page 174

  • Set k = k+1 and repeat from step 2.

  • Previously, we stated that the Quasi-Newton method satisfies the following condition:

M(i) λ(i) = δ(i)

  • Let’s see if the DFP method has this property.

  • From the last equation in step 5 above, we can state that:

M(k+1) λ(k+1) = M(k) λ(k+1) + A(k+1) λ(k+1) + B(k+1) λ(k+1)

  • But: A(k+1) λ(k+1) = \(\frac{\delta^{(k + 1)}{\delta^{(k + 1)}}^{T}\lambda^{(k + 1)}}{{\delta^{(k + 1)}}^{T}\lambda^{(k + 1)}}\) = δ(k+1)

and: B(k+1) λ(k+1) = \(- \left( \frac{M^{(k)}\lambda^{(k + 1)}{\lambda^{(k + 1)}}^{T}M^{(k)}\lambda^{(k + 1)}}{{\lambda^{(k + 1)}}^{T}M^{(k)}\lambda^{(k + 1)}} \right)\) = - M(k) λ(k+1)

  • Therefore:

M(k+1) λ(k+1) = M(k) λ(k+1) + δ(k+1) - M(k) λ(k+1)

⇒ M(k+1) λ(k+1) = δ(k+1)

as required!

  • Only first derivatives are required and there is no need to solve equations.

  • Generates conjugate directions on a positive definite quadratic function and, therefore, can find the minimum of such functions in at most n steps.

  • Converges to a minimum on a well-behaved convex function.

5.2.2 The BFGS Algorithm

  • The BFGS (Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb, Shanno) algorithm is very similar to the DFP algorithm, but provides an alternative formula for calculating M(k+1).

  • The BFGS algorithms follows exactly the same steps as outlined for the DFP, with the exception of Step 5, where the formula for calculating M(k+1) is as follows:

A(k+1) = \(\left( 1 + \frac{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}^{T}\mathbf{M}^{(k)}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}}{{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{(k+1)}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{(k+1)}} \right)\) \(\left( \frac{\delta^{(k + 1)}{\delta^{(k + 1)}}^{T}}{{\delta^{(k + 1)}}^{T}\lambda^{(k + 1)}} \right)\)

B(k+1) = \(- \left( \frac{\delta^{(k + 1)}{\lambda^{(k + 1)}}^{T}M^{(k)} + \text{ }M^{(k)}\lambda^{(k + 1)}{\delta^{(k + 1)}}^{T}}{{\delta^{(k + 1)}}^{T}\lambda^{(k + 1)}} \right)\)

M(k+1) = M(k) + A(k+1) + B(k+1)

  • Page 181

  • When both methods use exact line searches (i.e. we calculate the exact minimum along each search direction) then both BFGS and DFP give exactly the same sequence of points.

  • However, in practice, exact line searches are not often used. Instead, it is better to find the search line minimum very approximately and use this as the starting point for the next iteration. This is referred to as an inexact line search.

  • While this will likely require more iterations of the algorithm, overall it actually requires fewer function evaluations (which, as mentioned several times at this stage, is a good thing!).

  • The reason for this is because when we are far away from the actual local minimum, then the quality of the search direction may be quite poor. Therefore an approximate line search minimum can be just as good as the actual minimum and the resulting next search direction can be just as good. The difference here is that this information is achieved with significantly fewer function evaluations!

  • On the other hand, when we are close to the actual local minimum, then the final convergence is generally relatively fast, even with an inexact line search.

  • When inexact line searches are used, the BFGS and DFP algorithms give different results.

  • Page 182

  • In practice, it has been found that the BFGS gives better results with inexact line searches. Thus, it is the preferred algorithm and is typically used in most optimisation software packages today.

5.3 Wolfe’s Conditions & Inexact Line Searches

  • Recall that for an exact line search we want to obtain α to minimise F(x(k) + α p(k)).

  • The key issue with an inexact line search is determining a suitable criteria for stopping. In other words, we want to find a suitable value for α (not necessarily the minimum) that will allow us to continue searching in a new direction.

  • In general, and in order to guarantee convergence to a local minimum, the inexact line search terminates once we obtain a value for α that satisfies Wolfe’s conditions.

  • Condition I requires that the error reduction must not be too small and is tested by the expression:

F(x(k) + α p(k)) ≤ F(x(k)) + ε1α\({\mathbf{g}^{\mathbf{(k)}}}^{\mathbf{T}}\)p(k)

where ε1 is a small positive constant, ε1 < 1.

  • Page 183

  • Condition II requires that the step size must not be too small and is checked using:

\({\mathbf{g}^{\mathbf{(k + 1)}}}^{\mathbf{T}}\)p(k) ≥ ε2 \({\mathbf{g}^{\mathbf{(k)}}}^{\mathbf{T}}\)p(k)

where ε2 is a small positive constant such that ε1 < ε2 < 1.

  • Page 184

  • In practice, condition II is not checked as it requires new gradient information at each trial step. Instead, inexact line searches are designed so that excessively small steps are avoided.

6. The Least Squares Problem

  • All the optimisation techniques we have studied to date can be used to minimise (or maximise) fairly general functions.

  • However, in certain problems, the efficiency of such techniques can be improved upon by using special features that may exist in the problem.

  • Here, we are going to consider one such problem, which is referred to as the least squares problem.

  • The least squares problem is one that involves the minimisation of a function that is formed by a sum of squares of other functions and is a common problem in nonlinear curve and model fitting as well as nonlinear equation solving and some design problems.

  • The least squares minimisation problem takes the form:

min F(x) = \(\sum_{i = 1}^{m}{{f_{i}}^{2}(x)} = {f_{1}}^{2}(x) + {f_{2}}^{2}(x) + \text{...} + {f_{m}}^{2}(x)\)

Page 186

where the vector x has n components.

  • Example problem – Let’s say we have developed a model for some real process that depends on two parameters, x1 and x2, and is given by the function:

ym(t) = x1 exp(x2 t)

  • Let’s also say that we have actual real data from the process that is expressed as a set of m data points (ti, yi). Then our least squares minimisation problem becomes one of solving:

min F(x) = \(\sum_{i = 1}^{m}\left\lbrack y_{i} - (x_{1}\text{exp}(x_{2}t_{i})) \right\rbrack^{\text{ 2}}\)

  • This problem can be solved using any of the methods that we have studied so far. However, when m ≥ n, we can devise better methods to solve the problem.

6.1 The Gauss-Newton Method

  • Define the vector f as:

f = [f1(x) f2(x) … fm(x)] T

  • Page 187

  • Hence, the least squares minimisation problem can be expressed as:

min F(x) = \(\sum_{i = 1}^{m}{{f_{i}}^{2}(x)}\) = f Tf = f12(x) + f22(x) + … + fm2(x)

  • Differentiating F(x) gives:

\(\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}} = \text{2}\text{f}_{1}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}} + \text{2}\text{f}_{2}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{1}} + \text{...} + \text{2}\text{f}_{m}\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}} \)\[{\text{ } \vdots \text{ } = \text{ } \vdots \text{ } }{\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{n}} = \text{2}\text{f}_{1}\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{n}} + \text{2}\text{f}_{2}\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{n}} + \text{...} + \text{2}\text{f}_{m}\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{n}}}\]

  • In matrix form, this is expressed as:

\(\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{1}} \\ \vdots \\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{n}} \end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{1}} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{n}} & \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{n}} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{n}} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} f_{1} \\ \vdots \\ f_{m} \end{bmatrix}\)

or

∇F = 2 J Tf

  • Page 188

  • The m x n matrix J is referred to as the Jacobian matrix of F and has terms defined by:

\(J_{\text{ij}} = \frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}\) for i = 1, 2, … m and j = 1, 2, …n

i.e.: J = \(\begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{2}} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{n}} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}} & \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{2}} & \cdots & \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{n}} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Page 189

  • Differentiating a second time gives:

\[\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial{x_{1}}^{2}} = 2\left( \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}.\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}} + f_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{1}}{\partial{x_{1}}^{2}} \right) + 2\left( \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}.\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{1}} + f_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{2}}{\partial{x_{1}}^{2}} \right) + \text{...} + 2\left( \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}}.\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}} + f_{m}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial{x_{1}}^{2}} \right)\]

\[\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{1}} = 2\left( \frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}.\frac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial x_{1}} + f_{1}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{1}}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{1}} \right) + 2\left( \frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}.\frac{\partial f_{2}}{\partial x_{1}} + f_{2}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{2}}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{1}} \right) + \text{...} + 2\left( \frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{2}}.\frac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial x_{1}} + f_{m}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{m}}{\partial x_{2}\partial x_{1}} \right)\]

… and so on for all the other elements of the hessian matrix.

  • Dividing the above terms into two groups, one containing the product of first derivative terms and the other containing the second derivatives, we can express the full hessian matrix as follows:

∇2F = \(2\left( J^{T}J + \sum_{i = 1}^{m}{f_{i}\nabla^{2}f_{i}} \right)\)

  • Page 190

  • Note that ∇2 fi is the hessian matrix of the function fi.

  • At this stage the Gauss-Newton method makes the assumption that the second term in the above expression is negligible in comparison with the first.

  • This is often justified if:

1) the absolute values of fi are small (as they often are) at or near a minimum of F and/or

2) the values of ∇2 fi are small, as is true in many practical cases.

  • Hence: ∇2F ≈ \(2J^{T}J\)

  • Note that this expression involves only first derivatives!

  • Recall that the Newton’s search algorithm obtains the search direction as:

p = - H-1g or p = - (∇2F)-1∇F

and hence the update is given by:

x(k+1) = x(k) – α ( (∇2F)-1 ∇F ) (k)

p(k) = - (\({J^{(k)}}^{T}\)J(k)) -1 \({J^{(k)}}^{T}\)f(k)

  • Note also that we tend to avoid calculating the inverse of a matrix directly, as it is both very difficult and computationally intensive for large matrices.

  • Instead, we employ a technique such as Gauss elimination, Cramer’s Rule, etc to solve the set of equations given by:

\({J^{(k)}}^{T}\)J(k)p(k) = - \({J^{(k)}}^{T}\)f(k)

The Gauss-Newton Algorithm

  • Select a starting point x(0) and evaluate F(x(0)).

  • Starting with k = 0, solve the following set of equations to get p(k):

\({J^{(k)}}^{T}\)J(k)p(k) = - \({J^{(k)}}^{T}\)f(k)

  • Using a line search (need not be exact!) procedure, solve:

\(\underset{\alpha}{\text{min}}\)F(x(k) + αp(k)) to give α(k)

  • Page 193

  • Check for convergence. If ||x(k+1) – x(k)|| is sufficiently small or if F(x(k+1)) – F(x (k)) is sufficiently small, then stop. Otherwise, set k = k+1 and repeat the process.

6.2 The Levenberg-Marquart algorithm

  • Consider the following matrix:

J TJ + μI

where μ is a scalar parameter such that μ > 0.

  • Thus, we are simply adding the value μ to the main diagonal of J TJ.

  • The eigenvalues λ and eigenvectors ν of this matrix satisfy the property:

( J TJ + μI ) ν = λν

  • Therefore: J TJ ν = (λ - μ) ν = λ′ ν

  • Page 200

  • Now, λ′ ( = λ - μ) is an eigenvalue of J TJ and ν is an eigenvector.

  • Hence, adding μI to J TJ leaves the eigenvectors unchanged but adds μ to the eigenvalues (λ = λ′ + μ).

  • Thus the matrix J TJ + μI has minimum eigenvalues = μ > 0 and is therefore non-singular.

  • Note, a singular matrix is non-invertible, i.e. its determinant = 0. It also implies that one of its eigenvalues is 0. Thus if the matrix (J TJ + μI) has eigenvalues > 0, then it is non-singular!

  • Hence the following set of equations can always be solved:

(J TJ + μI)(k) p(k) = - ( J Tf )(k)

  • The Levenberg-Marquart algorithm uses this approach to compute the search directions.

Choosing a value for μ

  • If μ is very large then the search direction p is:

p \(\approx - \frac{1}{\mu}J^{T}f = - \frac{1}{\text{2}\text{μ}}\)∇F ( ∇F = 2 J Tf )

  • Thus, the search direction p tends towards the steepest descent and the || p || is very small.

  • On the other hand, if μ is small then p tends towards the Gauss-Newton search direction:

  • Page 202

  • Generally, μ is set to zero unless JTJ is singular, in which case it is set to a small value, typically:

μ = 10-4 \(\sum_{i = 1}^{n}\left( J^{T}J \right)_{\text{ii}}\) (i.e. the sum of the diagonal terms)

Condition Number

  • The condition number of JTJ is defined as:

C = \(\frac{\lambda_{\text{max}}}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}\)

  • C = ∞ for a singular matrix (i.e. one of the eigenvalues is zero, hence λmin = 0).

  • Generally, if C ≥ 104 (for example) then the matrix JTJ is said to be ill-conditioned and the Gauss-Newton search directions may be inaccurate.

  • Adding μI improves the conditioning:

\(\frac{\lambda_{\text{max}} + \mu}{\lambda_{\text{min}} + \mu}\) < \(\frac{\lambda_{\text{max}}}{\lambda_{\text{min}}}\) for μ > 0

6.3 Sensitivity Analysis

  • Consider the quadratic function:

F(x) = ½ xT H x,

where H = ∇2F is the positive-definite hessian matrix.

  • If F(x) is a sum of squares, then using the Gauss-Newton assumption:

H ≈ 2J TJ

  • For the ith eigenvalue λi and eigenvector νi of this matrix, we know that:

J TJ νi = λiνi for i = 1, 2, ...n

  • Page 204

  • Thus, we can write:

J TJ Q = QD

where: Q = [ν1 ν2 … νn] and D = \(\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_{1} & & & 0 \\ & \lambda_{2} & & \\ & & \ddots & \\ 0 & & & \lambda_{n} \end{bmatrix}\)

  • Multiplying both sides by QT gives:

J TJ QQT = QDQT

  • The eigenvectors of a real symmetric matrix (J TJ) are mutually orthogonal, and by normalising each eigenvector, Q becomes an orthonormal matrix, i.e.:

QTQ = I , i.e. Q-1 = QT

  • Therefore we obtain:

J TJ = QDQT

  • Page 205

  • Consider the transformation x = Qy. Here, y gives the position of x in the co-ordinate system formed by the eigenvectors of J TJ, as illustrated in the following 2-d example:

  • Note that:

|| x || = \(\sqrt{x^{T}x}\) = \(\sqrt{y^{T}Q^{T}Qy}\)= \(\sqrt{y^{T}y}\) = || y ||

  • Hence, the transformation leaves the lengths unchanged.

  • Now:

F(x) = ½ xT H x = xTJ TJ x = xTQDQTx

  • Substituting for x gives:

F(y) = yTQTQ D QTQ y

= yT D y

= \(\sum_{i = 1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}{y_{i}}^{2}}\)

  • A contour of F(y) has an equation of the form \(\sum_{i = 1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}{y_{i}}^{2}}\)= k, where k is a constant.

  • Page 207

  • This results in an ellipse in n-space, centred at the origin with its axes parallel to the
    co-ordinate axes (i.e. the eigenvectors of JTJ) and the length of its axes inversely proportional to \(\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\).

  • For example, consider the case for n = 2. Hence:

λ1 y12 + λ2 y22 = k

  • Compare this with the standard equation of an ellipse: \(\frac{{y_{1}}^{2}}{a^{2}} + \frac{{y_{2}}^{2}}{b^{2}} = 1\)

  • Hence: a = \(\sqrt{\frac{k}{\lambda_{1}}}\) and b = \(\sqrt{\frac{k}{\lambda_{2}}}\)

  • Therefore, a sensitivity analysis needs to be carried out. This involves computing the eigenvalues and the eigenvector of the matrix JTJ.

where νmin is the eigenvector associated with the eigenvalue λmin.

6.4 Solving Nonlinear Equations

  • Consider the problem of finding x ∈ ℜn such that:

fi(x) = 0 for i = 1, 2, … n

where fi(x) are nonlinear functions.

F(x) = \(\sum_{i = 1}^{n}{{f_{i}}^{2}(x)}\)

p = - J -1 \({\mathbf{J}^{\mathbf{T}}}^{\mathbf{-}\mathbf{1}}\)J Tf = - J -1f

x(k+1) = x(k) – α (J -1f )(k)
